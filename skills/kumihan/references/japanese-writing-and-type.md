# Japanese writing and type: decisions and limits

## Characters and fonts
Kanji and kana have historical development; modern font categories are not universal reading-performance rankings. Movable type, phototypesetting and digital fonts offer different production constraints. Use this knowledge to choose available, licensed faces and preserve supplied historical/variant forms, never to invent a logo etymology. See research-claims.json (H11/H12 and historical sources).

A codepoint sequence, abstract character form, actual glyph and font file are distinct. OpenType requests and fallback can change glyph output without changing source text. Record actual font/version/run, not merely a Pan-CJK family label. Comparing the regional form of 骨 needs an authorized reference and actual glyph observation; do not presume every face exposes the same distinction.

## Register and structure
Conversation, notices, explanations and brand copy serve different readers and tasks. Spoken/written register and prose/display are separate axes. A short notice may omit necessary shared context; a longer explanation may be clearer. Do not split supplied sentences to satisfy character counts or kanji percentages, or convert colloquial language into formal prose without authorization. Synthetic comparison only: 明日、こっちは17時で閉めます。 needs a known utterance date and place; do not infer them for a customer.

## Measurement and claims
Separate authored px/pt/Q, resolved font, rendered ink bounds, physical mm/viewing distance and reader performance. A line box is not ink height. M19's adopted sign comparison measures 木 and E (printed pp99–100 / PDF indices20–21), not the virtual body; this is not a universal legal minimum. Unknown projection conditions remain unchecked. Non-significant results do not establish zero effect. Preference, speed, accuracy and comprehension are different outcomes; manufacturer descriptions are not independent experiments. A UD label and a size table guarantee no reader performance.

## Historical reference for selection

Supplied research H01/H28 distinguishes phonographic and logographic uses of kanji: do not reduce kanji to pictures or standardize 香り / かおり / カオリ without authority. H02/H03 describes hiragana development from cursive man’yōgana and katakana from abbreviated kanji components; this does not support a single inventor or gender stereotype. Use curved strokes, kana proportions and rhythm as observable selection language instead.

H04 describes the coexistence of movable type, binding and woodblock images in Saga editions; H05 describes modern metal-type production, not the beginning of all Japanese printing. Manufacturer histories H08/H09 describe phototypesetting's optical transformation/spacing and digital redesign. These are historical/source descriptions, not independent performance experiments. Use them to distinguish revival, quotation and contemporary interpretation, and test actual content rather than rank technologies as a simple progress ladder.

H06/H07 provides broad mincho/gothic histories. Compare actual kana proportions, horizontal strokes, white space, weight families, mixed-script alignment, character coverage and licensing. Category does not predict ink size or distant reading. H16's written-language transition is not verbatim transcription: edited prose uses paragraphs and punctuation, while spoken utterances carry context absent from text. None of this authorizes rewriting supplied copy. See research-claims.json for exact URLs, source dates, classifications and limitations; these notes rely on the supplied research rather than a new independent source retrieval.
